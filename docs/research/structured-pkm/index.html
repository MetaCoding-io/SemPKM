Customer research

Do you use your PKM like a
personal database or information system?

I'm researching people who use tools like Obsidian, Tana, Capacities, Anytype, Notion, Logseq, and similar systems with real types of things, properties, relationships, queries, templates, or scripts. I want to understand how those systems actually evolve in practice — especially where they become powerful, fragile, or hard to maintain.

Volunteer for an Interview See if You're a Fit
This is research, not a sales call. I'm building SemPKM, but the first part of the conversation is about your current system: what you built, what changed over time, what you rely on, and what has become painful or surprisingly effective. You do not need to be unhappy with your current tool.
Who I'm looking for

You may be a good fit if your notes have become a system

You do not need to check every box. A few of these is enough to make the conversation useful.

You have different kinds of notes or objects — papers, people, projects, meetings, claims, clients, books, servers, experiments, or similar.
Different kinds of notes have different expected properties, frontmatter, fields, templates, or conventions.
You use Bases, Dataview, queries, dashboards, tables, or other ways to treat your notes like structured data.
You rely on plugins, scripts, regex, AI-generated code, or bulk-editing tools to keep your system consistent.
The kind of relationship matters to you — not just that two notes are linked, but whether one cites, supports, contradicts, contains, owns, or depends on another.
You've had to reorganize, migrate, rename properties, clean up old metadata, or change the model after hundreds of notes already existed.
You care about plain files, local ownership, exports, or whether your system will still make sense outside the app that created it.
You've tried a more object-oriented PKM like Tana, Capacities, or Anytype and either loved the structure, fought with it, or eventually left.
The most interesting question for me is not “is your setup complicated?” It's: if your setup became too complicated, would removing most of its structure actually solve the problem? If the answer is “no — I genuinely need the structure,” I especially want to talk.
What I'm trying to understand

The real work behind a structured PKM

I'm especially interested in concrete stories — the last migration, broken workflow, schema change, plugin decision, or system redesign — rather than abstract feature wish lists.

Model evolution

When your schema changes

What happens when every Book, Paper, Person, or Project suddenly needs a new property, or when an old property changes meaning?

Tooling

When plugins become infrastructure

Which plugins, scripts, templates, or queries are now essential? What would actually break if one disappeared?

Relationships

When a link isn't enough

Do you care whether a Paper cites a Source, a Claim contradicts another Claim, or a Server runs a Service? How do you represent that now?

Capture

When structure gets in the way

When does choosing a type, tag, folder, collection, or template interrupt thought? When does that structure actually help?

Portability

What survives when you leave?

Is it enough that your notes export, or do you also need relationships, schemas, queries, views, and other parts of the system to survive?

Counter-evidence

When simplification was the answer

If you once built an elaborate PKM and later stripped it back to plain notes, folders, or a few stable plugins, I want that story too.

What the conversation is like

No presentation. Mostly me asking you to show me how your system works.

The goal is to learn how you actually work, not to convince you that you should work differently.

First

Walk me through your setup

What kinds of things live in it? Which properties, templates, queries, plugins, and relationships matter?

Then

Tell me what changed

We'll focus on recent real events: restructuring a vault, changing metadata, switching apps, dropping plugins, or automating maintenance.

Finally

React to a few ideas

Only after I understand your workflow, I may show a few SemPKM concepts and ask what feels useful, irrelevant, or wrong.

You never need to expose private note content. Structure, screenshots with sensitive content hidden, or a few representative examples are enough. If a quote or screenshot would ever be useful publicly, I'll ask before attributing it.
Interested?

I'd like to see the system you've actually built.

Whether you love your current setup, are frustrated with it, or simplified after going too far, your experience can help shape what SemPKM becomes.

I'll use this only to decide whether a research conversation is a good fit. Nothing is attributed or shared without asking you first.
